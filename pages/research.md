**Environmental regulatory risk** <u style="color: #1a5fb4;"><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eneco.2026.109625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color: #1a5fb4;"><em>Energy Economics</em>, 2026</a></u>  \
With H.P. Boswijk, C. Diks, S. Trimborn  \
<span style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #555;"><em>Energy Economics</em>, Vol. 163, 109625 (November 2026) · <u style="color: #1a5fb4;"><a href="https://github.com/vallematteo/replication_env_reg_risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color: #1a5fb4;">Replication package</a></u></span>

<details style="margin-top: 8px;"><summary style="cursor: pointer; font-size: 0.9em; color: #555;">Abstract</summary>
<p style="font-size: 0.9em; line-height: 1.5; color: #555; margin-top: 8px;"> We determine from market expectations how firms are affected by environmental regulatory risk. We use a text-based measure of environmental regulatory stringency derived from US EPA legal documents and industry-level relevance scores to capture time-varying regulatory risk exposure. We find that environmental regulatory stringency carries a positive and statistically significant risk premium, especially for firms with high cash holdings. For firms with low cash holdings, the effect is highly volatile, showing investors are uncertain about a firms future when faced with stricter regulation. Firms' environmental profiles further matter, as high-emission firms returns are negatively affected when regulatory stringency increases. Because regulatory text is released infrequently, real-time risk analysis is challenging. We derive a high-frequency, market-expectation-capturing Environmental Regulatory Risk Index (ERRI). We show that ERRI captures shifts in investors' expectations of environmental regulatory risk and how it reacts during environmental policy and political developments.</p>
</details>

**Neighbourhood flood damage amplifies mortgage default cascades** <u style="color: #1a5fb4;"><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29652" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" style="color: #1a5fb4;"><em>arXiv</em></a></u>  \
With D. Kandhai, C. Diks, D. Roy

<details style="margin-top: 8px;"><summary style="cursor: pointer; font-size: 0.9em; color: #555;">Abstract</summary>
<p style="font-size: 0.9em; line-height: 1.5; color: #555; margin-top: 8px;"> Floods threaten mortgage repayment by damaging homes, disrupting incomes, and worsening conditions in surrounding neighbourhoods. However, it remains unclear how these neighbourhood effects turn local damage into systemic mortgage distress, as Europe’s housing crisis leaves households that lose their homes with few affordable alternatives. Using an agent-based model calibrated to Limburg, the Netherlands, we show that damage to surrounding properties can trigger mortgage defaults among households that can still afford to pay, including those whose own homes remain undamaged. In the most severe flood scenario, the annual foreclosure rate more than doubles. As each foreclosure lowers the value of nearby homes, it triggers a cascade. Floods make such large foreclosure cascades more frequent, with the share of foreclosures that belong to these cascades rising from 31% to 45%. They also shift foreclosures towards younger mortgages, when more debt is still outstanding. These findings show how neighbourhood damage extends mortgage risk beyond directly flooded homes and causes foreclosures to arrive in concentrated waves earlier in repayment.</p>
</details>
